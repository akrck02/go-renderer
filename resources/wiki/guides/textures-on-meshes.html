🧱 Textures on meshes

This guide is for a program that wants pictures on its 3D surfaces: a tiling detail (stones, panels, a grid), a picture it makes at run time, or the textures a model brings. At the end you will know how to give a material a texture, how it repeats, and what each target does with it.

A texture in four lines

cobbles, _ := scene.BuiltInTexture("cobbles")
square.Material = &scene.Material{
	BaseColor:    graphics.Vector{0.6, 0.58, 0.55, 1},
	Texture:      cobbles,
	TextureScale: [2]float32{0.5, 0.5}, // one repeat every two units
}

The surface’s colour is BaseColor × its vertex colours × the texture, lit as before. The built-in textures are grey, so BaseColor tints them: one texture serves a red brick wall and a grey one.

Where the texture lies comes from the mesh’s UVs, two numbers per vertex, as Positions has three: (0, 0) is the picture’s top-left corner and (1, 1) its bottom-right. A mesh without UVs takes them from where its vertices are, one unit of the model a repeat: x and z on what faces up, along the wall and up on walls (Mesh.SurfaceUVs). That is how a terrain or a box made in code can wear a tiling texture with no UVs at all.

How often it repeats is TextureScale, the repeats for each unit of UV along u and v (0 counts as 1), and TextureOffset slides it, in repeats.

The built-in textures

scene.BuiltInTexture(name) makes a texture in Go the first time it is asked for and keeps it, so every material that asks for one shares it. Each is a square, 64 or 128 pixels a side, that repeats without a seam (a test checks each edge runs into the other). Each has a Gain that brings its average to white, so a cobbled square is as bright as its BaseColor, as the old procedural patterns were; give a picture of your own a Gain for the same:

Surfaces Details
cobbles, masonry, plaster, shingles, planks, grass panels, grid, stripes, carbon, hexagons, worn, camouflage, windows

In a .glb, a material’s extras can ask for one: "texture": "planks", "textureScale": 2.

A picture of your own

Any decoded picture becomes a texture through draw2d.Image, the same type 2D lists draw:

picture, err := draw2d.DecodeImage(file) // a PNG, JPEG or GIF
if err != nil {
	return err
}
poster := &scene.Texture{Picture: picture}

Give it to as many materials as you like: each backend uploads its pixels once. To show other pixels, write them into picture.Pixels and call picture.Changed(): the texture is uploaded again, once, and every mesh that shows it changes. A picture’s colours are sRGB, as a PNG’s are, and come out as they went in under white light.

Setting Values Default
Wrap scene.Repeat, scene.Clamp, scene.Mirror Repeat
Sharp nearest pixel (pixel art) instead of blended smooth
Format on the 3DS: scene.RGBA8, scene.RGB565, scene.ETC1 RGBA8

Models that bring their own

loaders.LoadScene and ReadScene read a glTF’s TEXCOORD_0 and its materials’ base colour texture: a PNG or JPEG inside the .glb, in a data URI, or beside a .gltf (LoadScene only). The sampler’s wrapping along u becomes Wrap, and a nearest magnification Sharp. Two materials that name the same texture share one scene.Texture.

On each target

Target What it does
OpenGL (desktop) sRGB textures with smaller copies for distance (mipmaps), sampled as each texture asks
OpenGL ES (browser, Android) The same; a picture whose sides are not powers of two is clamped, without smaller copies, as OpenGL ES 2 requires
Metal The same as the desktop; written without a Mac, still to be built and looked at on one
Nintendo 3DS Sides scaled to powers of two from 8 to 1024; RGBA8, RGB565 or ETC1; the texture multiplies the baked light. Water and moving nodes draw without it. See the 3DS guide

A scene without textures costs what it did before them: UVs live in a buffer of their own, made only for meshes drawn with a texture (see the optimization plan).
