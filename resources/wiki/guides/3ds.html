🎮 On the Nintendo 3DS

Status: a first step (October 2026). Package ctr draws through the 3DS’s graphics chip, the PICA200, with citro3d: cmd/triangle3ds turns a triangle of three colours on the top screen at 60 frames a second, and cmd/island3ds flies round the sample island with ctr.SceneRenderer. It runs as homebrew, from the Homebrew Launcher, built with a TinyGo that has a nintendo3ds target (a separate fork of TinyGo, not part of this repository). The triangle is checked in the Azahar emulator; the loading and planning figures below come from a New 3DS XL.

How it fits together

Layer What it does
Your program Plans frames with sceneplan, moves things with graphics
ctr The top screen, vertex shaders, vertex buffers and matrices, over citro3d (this package)
citro3d, libctru devkitPro’s libraries for the GPU and the system, reached through cgo
TinyGo nintendo3ds Builds the program as a .3dsx for the Homebrew Launcher

The rest of go-renderer is plain Go and already runs there: on the console, encoding/json, image/png, loaders.ParseSceneGLB and the sample island load, and planning a frame of the island takes under a millisecond. The tests of graphics, scene, sceneplan, camera, draw2d, input and frameloop pass in the emulator.

What is different about the PICA200

  • No fragment shaders. Vertex shaders are written in the PICA200’s assembly (.v.pica) and assembled with devkitPro’s picasso; colours come out of a few fixed stages. ctr sets the first one to pass the vertex colour through.
  • Matrices row by row, components backwards. citro3d stores a matrix as four rows, and each row as w, z, y, x. graphics.Mat4 is column by column; ctr.PICAMatrix lays it out (tested on any computer).
  • A screen on its side. The 3DS’s screens are portrait panels turned a quarter turn, and depth runs from -1 to 0. ctr.OrthographicTilt is citro3d’s Mtx_OrthoTilt for a right-handed view, as everywhere in go-renderer: what is visible lies between z = 0 and z = -far. cmd/triangle3ds checks it against citro3d’s own on the console.
  • Vertex buffers outside the Go heap. ctr.NewVertexBuffer copies vertices to the linear memory the GPU reads, so the garbage collector never scans them.

The scene renderer

ctr.SceneRenderer draws a scene.Scene the way gles does: lit meshes and instances, the light of the time of day, fog and translucent water, planned by sceneplan. What changes on the PICA200:

  • Light worked out on the processor. Lighting every vertex on the GPU held the sample island to 30 frames a second on a New 3DS XL: the GPU took 34 ms a frame, the processor 0.4 ms. Sun and sky change slowly, so ctr/bake.go lights each vertex once, when a mesh is uploaded (sun, sky and ground light, glow, flat by brightness, then the screen’s gamma), and keeps its colour in four bytes: a vertex is 16 bytes instead of 40. A mesh is baked again only when the light, the mesh or its node’s turn changes. ctr/shaders/baked.v.pica then only places each vertex and fogs it by its distance to the camera, linearly: 27 instructions instead of 95.
  • Water on the GPU, on a coarser grid. Water’s reflection of the sky depends on where the camera looks from, so water goes through the full shader, ctr/shaders/scene.v.pica (gles’s scene shader done per vertex, with its smoothstep fog and the chip’s lg2 and ex2 for gamma). The planner’s water grid of 256 × 256 cells, made for the desktop’s waves, was 131 072 of the island’s 160 756 triangles; ctr asks for 32 × 32 (Planner.WaterGridDivisions), still fine near the camera for the fog.
  • Not the chip’s own fog. The PICA200 can fog in fixed function, from a table of 128 steps of the depth buffer; but depth is far from linear (with the near plane at 0.1 and the far one at 20 000, the whole island’s fog would fall in the first step), so ctr fogs per vertex.
  • OpenGL’s matrices, turned. sceneplan plans frames with OpenGL’s clip space; one fixed matrix (ctr.PICAViewProjection) gives the PICA200’s quarter turn and depth range. Turning graphics.OrthographicOpenGL with it gives ctr.OrthographicTilt (tested on any computer).
  • 16-bit indices. Meshes with more than 65 535 vertices are drawn in pieces (ctr/split.go).
  • Instances merged on the processor, as in gles: the merging moved to sceneplan (MergedBatch), which both renderers share.
  • Meshes in linear memory, uploaded once and again only when Dirty.

The bottom screen

ctr.FlatRenderer paints a draw2d.List (an interface: go-ui’s, or a program’s own) on the bottom screen, as gles.FlatRenderer does in browsers and on phones: opengl/overlay lays the list out as rectangles of one atlas of glyphs, icons and rounded corners, and they are drawn in one call. On the PICA200 the atlas is a texture of one byte per pixel (A8), which has to be laid out in the chip’s own way: tiles of 8 × 8 pixels, from the bottom of the picture, Morton order inside each tile (ctr/texture.go, tested on any computer); a colour stage then multiplies each rectangle’s alpha by the atlas’s coverage. Pictures (draw2d images) are the overlay’s second atlas, of straight RGBA pixels, tiled the same way as an RGBA8 texture (whose bytes the chip reads as alpha, blue, green, red); their rectangles multiply the picture by their colour and then by the coverage, which rounds their corners. Rectangles are drawn in runs, with or without a picture, in the list’s order, so what is painted over what stays the same. Both atlases have to stay within the chip’s 1024 × 1024 pixels, and a picture that changes uploads the whole pictures atlas again. The chip has no fragment shader to read one picture sharp and another blended, so a sharp picture (draw2d.Image’s Sharp, for pixel art) makes its whole list read the pictures atlas at the nearest pixel: a game’s top screen is sharp, the interface below stays smooth.

Both screens are drawn in one frame: ctr.BeginFrame, then SceneRenderer.PaintStereo for the top screen and FlatRenderer.Paint for the bottom one, then ctr.EndFrame. (Draw and DrawStereo begin and end a frame themselves, for a program with the top screen only.) go-3ds puts go-ui on the bottom screen this way.

Both screens, and the controls

ctr.Surface is either screen: SceneRenderer.PaintOn and FlatRenderer.PaintOn draw a scene or an interface on the top one or the bottom one (which has a depth buffer, so a scene can be shown where it can be touched). go-ui’s window for the 3DS uses it to swap the screens with SELECT.

ctr.ReadControls reads the buttons, the circle pad and the touch screen, and ctr.Controls turns them into input.State: the cross is the arrow keys, L and R minus and plus, A Enter, B Escape and the system’s back, the circle pad the stick (input.State.StickX and StickY) and the touch screen the pointer (tested on any computer). The cameras read the stick as a game’s player moves: the orbit and the plan move their target over the ground, the walk walks.

ctr.Open asks a New 3DS for its fast mode (804 MHz and the second-level cache): libctru leaves the processor at 268 MHz otherwise, a third of what it can do.

FlatRenderer keeps the corners of every list it paints, one buffer per list painted in a frame. A list that draws the same as the frame before (its fingerprint says so) is drawn again from them, without being laid out: an interface that does not change costs the processor almost nothing. Each rectangle is four corners and two triangles of a fixed index buffer, written straight into linear memory.

ctr.ReadMemory tells how much of the console’s memory is free: the linear memory the GPU reads, the video memory and the application’s region, which holds the Go heap. It is cheap enough to read every second, for a program that shows its own resources.

Memory

TinyGo’s collector does not move what it keeps, so garbage made over and over fragments the heap until a large block no longer fits. ctr keeps its bake and upload buffers and reuses them, and lets go of a mesh, on the GPU and in its caches, once it has not been drawn for a second: a program that makes its scene again (a plan whose machines change colour) leaves nothing behind.

Keeping a frame short

Measure before changing anything: go-ui’s window.LastFrameTimes splits a frame into waiting for the GPU, painting the scene, what is drawn over it, the interface, and handing the frame over. The processor’s time to prepare an interface is not where it costs; laying it out as rectangles is.

  • The overlay costs by the rectangle. FlatRenderer lays a list out only when it draws something else than the frame before; an interface that stands still costs almost nothing. A part that changes every frame (a clock, an animation, figures read every second) makes its whole list be laid out again: keep it small, or update it seldom.
  • Pixel-art shapes are many rectangles. A window with an outline, a frame, rounded corners in steps and a shadow is fifteen or more; a key with a lip, twenty. They are cheap while they stand still and dear on the frames they change. A graph is two rectangles a column (the filled part and the track), not one a block.
  • The scene’s own drawing goes in both eyes. With 3D on, World.Overlay.Drawing is painted twice: keep it to a sight and a label.
  • Waiting for the GPU is the time the PICA200 took for the frame before: triangles, filled pixels and blending. Fewer triangles far away, and fewer layers of transparent rectangles, lower it.
  • The New 3DS’s fast mode is on: ctr.Open asks for it.

Garbage

TinyGo’s collector stops the program while it runs, so a frame should make as little garbage as it can. ctr, the overlay and the scene planner make none in a steady frame: their lists and buffers are kept and filled again. What a program builds every frame is the rest; tinygo-3ds’s NINTENDO3DS.md lists what makes garbage in TinyGo that would not in Go, and how to find it.

In 3D

The top screen shows a picture for each eye when the 3D slider is up. SceneRenderer.DrawStereo plans the frame once, for the camera between the eyes, and draws it twice: each eye’s camera is moved sideways by half the separation, looking the same way, and its picture is shifted so that what lies at the focal distance falls on the plane of the screen (ctr.EyeViewProjection, tested on any computer); nearer comes out of the screen, farther goes in. ctr.Slider3D reads the slider; a program usually makes the separation grow with it. Drawing twice doubles the GPU’s work: the sample island goes from about 5 to 10 ms a frame.

Building

tinygo build -target=nintendo3ds -o triangle3ds.3dsx ./cmd/triangle3ds

Copy the .3dsx to /3ds/ on the SD card and open it from the Homebrew Launcher.

Not yet

  • How fast the renderer is on the console with the coarser water (baking the light alone went from 30 to 36 frames a second: the chip was held back by the number of triangles more than by the shader). Positions in shorts and fewer triangles far away are the next steps if it is not enough.
  • A sun that moves all the time bakes the meshes again whenever a colour would change by a step.
  • Reading scenes from the SD card: TinyGo’s os has no file system there yet, so scenes are embedded in the program.
  • Collection pauses: about 170 ms in the emulator for an 80 MB heap with 30 MB alive. Making little garbage every frame keeps them rare.
