🍎 Metal: a backend for flat drawing

Status: built (October 2026): the flat path, which Go UI’s window draws through on macOS by default, and the 3D scene renderer, which the viewer draws through on macOS by default (see The 3D scene renderer below). This page started as the design; What was built and Measured say how it turned out. Pending a look on screen: live resizing by dragging an edge, and a second screen with another scale.

Why

OpenGL has been deprecated on macOS since 2018 and is frozen at version 4.1. On Apple silicon it runs as a translation layer over Metal (the driver reports “4.1 Metal”). It still ships in macOS 26. Apple has announced no removal, but there are warning signs: a report from a macOS 27 beta of an OpenGL application that would not start, and Chrome, which dropped its OpenGL backend on macOS and draws only through Metal.

It is not needed for performance or battery today. An interface that draws on demand sleeps while idle, and each frame it does draw is one draw call; the translation layer costs about 0.1–0.2 ms of such a frame. Metal is insurance, plus a few things OpenGL cannot do on macOS: smooth live resizing (presentsWithTransaction), choosing the low-power GPU, and pacing frames with the display.

Where it differs from the design below:

  • An instance is 36 bytes, not 32: the screen and atlas rectangles stay as floats (the atlas places glyphs at half pixels), and the colour is four bytes.
  • The layer keeps two drawables (maximumDrawableCount = 2) instead of three: a window that draws on demand never queues more than one frame, and each drawable is a whole window of pixels.
  • A new atlas texture each time the atlas changes, instead of replaceRegion on the changed rectangle: a frame still being drawn keeps reading the old texture, and the atlas changes only when glyphs or corners are added. Uploading only the changed rectangle is left for later, as on the OpenGL path.
  • The OpenGL path keeps its six vertices per quad for now; moving it to the same instances is step 5 of Go UI’s performance audit.

Measured

On a laptop with an Apple M4 and a 60 Hz screen, macOS 26, a 1280 × 800 window on a high-density screen.

Captures: the register application captured through both backends differs in fewer than 1 % of its pixels, by at most 1⁄255 in a channel (the colour rounded to a byte), light and dark. Go UI’s cmd/app test TestMetalDrawsLikeOpenGL checks it.

The register application, idle (drawing on demand):

Most of the memory saved is the window’s buffers and the driver’s caches: OpenGL on macOS runs on a translation layer over Metal, which keeps more buffers and caches of its own.

The 3D scene renderer

Built after the flat path, in the same way:

  • One plan, two backends. What a frame draws (commands in order, culling, levels of detail, instance batches, the shadow and seabed regions) moved out of opengl into sceneplan, which makes no graphics calls. OpenGL’s captures came out identical, pixel for pixel, before and after the move. metal.SceneRenderer draws the same plan.
  • Shaders translated one to one (metal/scene_shaders.go), compiled at start with precise maths: the noise behind the patterns, stars and clouds takes sines of large numbers, and fast maths would round them differently. The uniform blocks are mirrored in Go byte for byte, checked by tests on both sides.
  • A thin Objective-C layer (scene_darwin.m): buffers, textures, pipelines, passes and draw calls; the logic stays in Go. A frame waits for the previous one, so buffers can be rewritten in place (a simulation moving instances).
  • Memoryless depth buffer where the GPU allows it (Apple silicon): nothing reads it after the frame, so it takes no memory.
  • The viewer chooses with -backend (Metal on macOS, falling back to OpenGL), and the visor opens a built-in sample island (-sample island) and draws at a fixed time (-time) for captures that can be compared.

Scope

First: the flat path, FlatRenderer — everything an interface-only window needs (Go UI’s window package). It is small: about 25 OpenGL calls, two short shaders, one atlas texture and one draw call a frame.

Later (now done, see above): the 3D scene renderer (shadows, sky, water, levels of detail, instancing; about 3400 lines and 660 lines of GLSL). That is three to six weeks and a decision of its own (see Options).

Options

Option Fit
Native Metal through cgo and Objective-C cgo is already required by GLFW, so nothing new. Smallest and most direct; how Gio draws on Apple systems. Chosen for the flat path.
Metal through purego (no cgo) How Ebitengine does it. No gain while GLFW needs cgo anyway, and the bindings are ours to write.
WebGPU through wgpu-native One renderer for Metal, Vulkan, OpenGL ES and Direct3D, with WGSL shaders: attractive for the 3D renderer and Android. A large native library and young Go bindings. Reconsider when porting the 3D renderer.

Android keeps its own small OpenGL ES flat renderer, as planned in Go UI’s docs/android.md.

How it fits together

GLFW Creates the window; with Metal it creates no OpenGL context (NoAPI)

The painter lives in opengl/overlay but imports nothing from OpenGL, so the Metal renderer uses it as it is. Moving it to a neutral package (for example draw2d/paint) is a tidy-up for later.

The pieces

1. The window and the layer (metal/, Objective-C through cgo)

  • GLFW creates the window with glfw.ClientAPI set to glfw.NoAPI: no OpenGL context.
  • window.GetCocoaWindow() gives the NSWindow; its content view gets a CAMetalLayer (wantsLayer = YES, layer = metalLayer).
  • The layer:
    • device: the system’s default device, or the low-power one when there is a choice.
    • pixelFormat: BGRA8Unorm.
    • framebufferOnly = YES, opaque = YES, contentsScale = the screen’s backing scale.
    • displaySyncEnabled = YES, unless vsync is off.
    • drawableSize follows the framebuffer size callback. The scale follows the window when it moves to another screen.
  • Live resize: while the window is being resized, presentsWithTransaction = YES and the frame is drawn from the refresh callback (as the OpenGL loop already does). This presents the new size in the same transaction as the window’s frame, so nothing stretches.

2. The flat renderer (metal.FlatRenderer)

It has the same method as the OpenGL one:

func NewFlatRenderer(window *glfw.Window, vsync bool) (*FlatRenderer, error)
func (renderer *FlatRenderer) Draw(list *draw2d.List, background draw2d.Color, width, height int, scale float32)
  • One pipeline: the vertex and fragment functions, with blending set in the pipeline the same way as the OpenGL path’s BlendFuncSeparate.
  • Instanced quads: about 32 bytes each (screen rectangle, atlas rectangle, colour), drawn as a four-vertex triangle strip per instance. Today’s OpenGL path uses 192 bytes per quad, so the OpenGL path should take the same layout at the same time and both stay alike.
  • Vertex buffers: three, used in turn and guarded by a semaphore, so the CPU never writes one the GPU is still reading. A buffer grows by doubling and is kept between frames.
  • The atlas: an R8Unorm texture. replaceRegion uploads only the rectangle that changed; a larger texture is made only when the atlas grows.
  • The shaders: about 30 lines of Metal Shading Language: position to clip space, atlas coverage times colour. They are compiled from source when the renderer starts (newLibraryWithSource), so building needs no Xcode, only the command-line tools.
  • The frame:
    1. take the next drawable;
    2. a render pass that clears to the background;
    3. one draw call;
    4. present;
    5. commit.

No depth buffer.

3. A loop shared by both backends

Today the window loop lives in opengl.StartLoop. Its parts are not about OpenGL:

  • GLFW events,
  • drawing on demand,
  • frame requests and Wake,
  • the resize follower,
  • cursor shape and lock,
  • the fixed-step clock.

They move to a package both backends use. Each backend gives the loop a small interface to make its window and to draw and show a frame. The OpenGL backend keeps its behaviour, and its tests must pass unchanged.

The existing hand-written metal.Metal and metal.MetalRenderer stubs (the immediate-mode Renderer contract) are left as they are. Filling them in is a separate decision for the author.

4. Captures without a screen

-capture draws into an off-screen texture with shared storage and reads it back into a PNG, as the OpenGL path does with ReadPixels. The same frame captured through both backends must match within a small tolerance. That comparison is the main test of the port.

5. Choosing the backend (Go UI)

  • window.Options.Backend: Automatic (Metal on macOS when a device exists, else OpenGL), Metal or OpenGL.
  • The flag -backend forces one.
  • If Metal fails to start, the window falls back to OpenGL and says so once.

Iterations

It was estimated at three to four days. Each step kept the OpenGL path working.

Testing

  • Without a window: unit tests for packing quads into instances, for the dirty rectangle of the atlas, and for choosing the backend.
  • With a window: captures through both backends compared pixel by pixel; the register idle for a minute (no frames, no processor); resizing by dragging an edge; a second screen with another scale.
  • Measured against OpenGL: processor time while moving the pointer, GPU use, memory footprint.

Risks

  • Pacing: nextDrawable blocks when every drawable is in use. Drawing on demand never holds one while idle. Continuous mode relies on display sync, and on skipping frames with no update.
  • Retina and scale changes: the layer’s contentsScale and drawableSize must follow the window as it moves between screens, or text turns blurry. This is tested on a second screen.
  • Two backends to keep alike: the captures comparison catches drift, and both share the painter and the quad layout.
  • OpenGL removal may never come: the work is still small, and it brings the resize and power gains with it.

Decisions

  1. Native Metal for the flat path and WebGPU only for a later 3D port. Taken.
  2. Shaders compiled from source at start-up rather than shipped as a compiled library. Taken: it needs no Xcode, and costs a few milliseconds at start.
  3. Metal as the default on macOS once captures match. Taken, with -backend opengl to go back.
  4. The OpenGL flat path takes the same instanced quads. Still to do (step 5 of the audit).
