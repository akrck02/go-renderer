🏷️ The overlay: labels, header and notices

Sometimes a picture needs words. Which boat is the ferry? What time is it in the harbour? Did the lighthouse just switch on? The overlay answers those questions with small, light labels drawn over the 3D view, a header pinned to the top-left corner and short notices that fade away.

It is visual only: nothing on it can be clicked. It is part of the scene (Scene.Overlay), so any program that changes the scene can change it, and headless captures (visor -capture) include it.

Labels

A scene.Label floats above something in the world:

Field Meaning
Node The node it follows: it moves with the node’s world transform and hides with it
Point Where it stands when there is no Node (a world position)
Height How far above the anchor, in world units: the top of a box, the roof of a house
Text A short text, cut with “…” when it is very long
Icon The name of a Lucide icon (anchor, sailboat, clock…), drawn before the text or alone in a circle
Badge A very short text in a small pill under the label: 7/10, open
Tint Colour of the icon and the badge, as on a web page (scene.ColorFromHex("#2563eb")); the text stays dark
Hidden Keep it, but do not draw it 
lighthouse := &scene.Label{Node: lighthouseNode, Height: 30, Text: "Lighthouse", Icon: "lighthouse"}
world.Overlay.AddLabel(lighthouse)
lighthouse.Badge = "lit"                 // change it whenever you like
world.Overlay.RemoveLabel(lighthouse)

Labels keep the same size on screen however far the camera is, and they are drawn only when their anchor is in front of the camera and inside the window. When two labels would cover each other, the one nearer the camera wins and the other waits until there is room: a crowded view stays readable instead of turning into a pile of pills. Labels also stay clear of the header and the notices.

The header and the notices

Overlay.Header is a list of lines (scene.HeaderLine: icon, text, tint) shown in a card at the top-left corner; the first line is a little bigger and bolder, for a clock or a title.

Notices are short messages under the header:

world.Overlay.AddNotice("anchor", "Boat 4 moored at the north quay", graphics.Vec4{})

They show newest first, at most MaximumNotices at a time (5 by default), for NoticeDuration seconds (6 by default), fading out over their last second. Their time is the overlay’s own Clock, which the viewer advances at every step, so a paused or slowed program does not lose its notices early.

Changing it while the scene is shown

viewer.Run calls your update function on the same goroutine that draws, between frames, so the update function can add, change and remove labels, lines and notices freely, with no locking.

From a glTF file

A file can carry labels too, in its node extras (see glTF extras): label, icon, badge, label_color and pinned. It is a handy way to try the overlay with cmd/visor alone.

How it is drawn

The overlay is the last pass of the OpenGL scene renderer, after the scene, the sky and the water:

  1. Layout (package opengl/overlay, plain Go, no graphics context needed): every label’s anchor is projected to the screen, the labels are sorted nearest first, and the header, notices and labels are cut into rectangles of one atlas.
  2. The atlas is a single grey image holding coverage masks: the glyphs of the text (Go Medium and Go Bold, from golang.org/x/image/font/gofont), the icons (the Lucide icon font) and the rounded corners of the pills (a disc, a ring and a soft shadow of each size). Pictures are drawn into it the first time they are needed, at the exact pixel size they are shown at, so text stays crisp; ASCII, Latin-1 and a few signs (→ … € •) are drawn as soon as a size is first used.
  3. Drawing: the atlas is uploaded when it changed, and every rectangle becomes two triangles in one draw call, in screen pixels, with alpha blending and no depth test. Pills of any width are built from the disc as nine pieces: four corners that keep their size and stretched edges.

Other scripts. The Go fonts (or a program’s own, UseFonts) have Latin, Greek and Cyrillic. A character they lack (Chinese, Arabic, Devanagari, a symbol) is drawn with one of the system’s fonts, as a system’s own text is: FontFolders are Android’s /system/fonts, or the desktop’s usual folders. The font is found by the name of its file, which names its script (NotoNaskhArabic, NotoSansCJK, from Go’s unicode tables); a character of no named font is looked for in every file under 8 MB, the last resort. Files are read the first time a character needs them, and a font found is kept, shared by every painter and measurer. Letters are drawn one after another, left to right: Arabic letters are not joined yet, and right-to-left text is not turned round.

Emoji are drawn in their own colours, from the system’s colour emoji font: each is a PNG in the font’s CBDT table, found through CBLC and cmap, which colour.go reads (x/image does not). An emoji becomes a picture in the picture atlas, scaled once to each size it is drawn at, and goes in the same draw call as the text, faded as the text is. An emoji by default (a face, a pictograph, transport) is drawn as a picture before any outline of it; another character is when it is followed by U+FE0F (❤️) or when no font has an outline of it. The variation selectors and the zero width joiner are not drawn; sequences joined by it (a family, a skin tone) show as their parts, as the font’s ligatures are not read. Android 13 and later draw their own emoji from vector outlines (COLRv1, not read here) and keep the pictures in NotoColorEmojiLegacy, which is used; the fonts are tried in turn, the one for flags alone last. Tried on the Android 14 emulator: “North light 🌊 😀 🚀 ❤️ 灯台” in one field.

On a high-density screen one window pixel is two framebuffer pixels; the viewer sets SceneRenderer.OverlayScale so the overlay keeps its size.

The colours aim for calm and contrast: white pills at 92 % opacity with a thin border and a soft shadow, dark text (#18212e), and colour only where it means something (the icon and the badge).

The icons

The icons come from Lucide (ISC licence), copied from the lucide-static npm package into opengl/overlay/lucide: the font lucide.ttf, its licence and a generated table from names to characters. opengl/overlay/lucide/generate.go explains how to update them. An unknown icon name draws nothing and is reported once in the log.

Free drawing: Overlay.Drawing

Labels, the header and notices are laid out by the renderer. For anything else drawn over the view (panels, buttons, a status bar) a program fills a draw list of package draw2d every frame and sets world.Overlay.Drawing to it once. It is painted last, over the labels.

var drawing draw2d.List
world.Overlay.Drawing = &drawing

// in the update function, every step:
drawing.Reset()
card := draw2d.Rect{X: 24, Y: 24, Width: 220, Height: 64}
drawing.Shadow(card, 10, 10, 3, draw2d.Hex("#14202f2e"))
drawing.Rectangle(card, 10, draw2d.Hex("#ffffff"), draw2d.Hex("#dde3ea"), 1)
drawing.Icon("lightbulb", 40, 48, 16, draw2d.Hex("#4a5768"))
drawing.Text("The lamp is lit", 64, 61, 13, draw2d.Regular, draw2d.Hex("#14202f"))
Command What it draws
Rectangle(area, radius, fill, border, width) A rectangle with rounded corners, a fill and a border inside its area
Shadow(area, radius, blur, drop, colour) A soft shadow for a rectangle, fading out over blur pixels and falling drop pixels
Text(text, x, baseline, size, weight, colour) One line of text in Go Regular, Medium or Bold
Icon(name, x, y, size, colour) A Lucide icon in a square
Image(picture, area, radius, tint) A picture covering a rounded rectangle, cropped evenly where their shapes differ; white tints nothing
PushClip(area) / PopClip() Cuts what follows to a rectangle (nested clips intersect)
SetLayer(layer) Higher layers cover lower ones, whatever order they were added in

Commands wholly outside the current clip are left out as they are added, so the rows of a long scrolled list out of sight cost nothing to paint; a text goes when its line misses the clip above or below, or starts past its right edge. Fingerprint(seed) sums up a list in one number: a window that draws on demand compares it with the last frame’s to skip a frame that would look the same.

Coordinates are layout pixels from the top-left corner, the same units as the cursor in input.State; on a high-density screen the renderer doubles them, so sizes match on every screen and text is drawn at its real pixel size. Shapes use the same atlas as the labels (the disc, ring and shadow pictures, cut into nine pieces), so a whole interface is still one draw call. Clipping is done on the processor: each rectangle is cut, and its atlas coordinates with it.

To lay text out before drawing it, overlay.NewMeasurer() measures a line exactly as it will be painted (MeasureText(text, size, weight, scale)); it needs no graphics context. Interface libraries such as Go UI build on these two pieces.

Pictures

A picture is a draw2d.Image: draw2d.DecodeImage(reader) reads a PNG, JPEG or GIF (a file the program embeds), draw2d.NewImage(picture) takes an image.Image, and draw2d.NewBlankImage makes one whose pixels the program writes itself. Keep it between frames: its pixels are uploaded once. To show other pixels in it (a video’s next frame), write them into Pixels and call Changed; only its own area is uploaded again. Pixel art sets Sharp: scaled up, its pixels stay square blocks instead of blending into their neighbours. The shaders read such a picture at the middle of its nearest pixel; the quads say so by adding graphics.SharpPictureOffset to their picture position, so every other picture is read as before and the vertices keep their layout.

Pictures sit side by side in a second atlas, of colours (overlay.Pictures, RGBA, made with the first picture). A picture’s quads read their colours there and their coverage in the grey atlas, as any rounded rectangle does, so corners and clips work as for every other shape and the whole interface stays one draw call. Photos larger than 2048 pixels are scaled down to it as they are copied in; the atlas grows to 4096 pixels, and when even that is full it is emptied and holds only the pictures of the frame being drawn.

A program that is only an interface, with no scene, uses opengl.FlatRenderer: it clears the window to a background colour and paints a draw list, with the same atlas and one draw call.

Frosted glass: backdrops

A translucent panel over a busy picture reads better when what is behind it is blurred, as frosted glass. Backdrop asks for that, just before the panel’s own rectangle:

panel := draw2d.Rect{X: 24, Y: 24, Width: 280, Height: 160}
drawing.Backdrop(panel, 12, 24)                                        // blur what is behind by 24 pixels
drawing.Rectangle(panel, 12, draw2d.Hex("#ffffff10"), draw2d.Transparent, 0) // the pane over it

The panel’s area now shows the frame behind it, blurred, with the pane’s faint white on top.

How it is drawn. The painter does not blur anything itself. It marks the backdrop’s quads, and before the first backdrop of each layer it asks the backend for a snapshot (Painter.Snapshots). The backend stops drawing there, copies the frame so far, shrinks it to half a few times and blurs the smallest copy in two passes, across and down. Then it goes on drawing, and every backdrop of that layer reads the blurred copy where it sits on screen.

What it costs, and how that is kept small:

  • Nothing, without backdrops. A list with none is still one draw call, with one more value per vertex.
  • One blur per layer, not per panel. Ten panels of one layer share a snapshot. The price is that a panel does not blur another panel of its own layer; a dialog, on a layer above, blurs the panels under it.
  • The blur runs on a small picture. For a 24-pixel blur the copy is halved four times, to a sixteenth of the screen’s side, so the blur itself works on under half a percent of the pixels; halving is a cheap, smooth average of four pixels into one. What is left is moving the frame once: on OpenGL it is halved straight from the frame (a multisampled frame is copied first), on OpenGL ES 2 copied first, as ES 2 cannot copy and shrink at once.
  • The textures are kept. They are made the first time and again only when the window changes size.
  • Metal reads the drawable directly, which needs the layer’s framebufferOnly off on every frame: a small cost of its own, frosted or not.

Measured with OpenGL drawn by the processor (llvmpipe, no graphics card: the slowest case), a frame of ten frosted panels over stripes, against the same frame without backdrops:

Window Without backdrops With backdrops
1280 × 760 3.1 ms 5.1 ms
2560 × 1520 7.0 ms 13.5 ms

A graphics card does the same work in a fraction of that. An interface that draws only when something changes, as go-ui’s window does, pays it only on the frames it draws; over a 3D scene, which draws every frame, it is paid every frame. A blur of 0 asks for no backdrop at all, so a system that asks for less transparency costs nothing.

Not done yet: Metal blurs a flat interface’s backdrops but not those over a 3D scene, which it leaves out (the panels show unblurred); and the snapshot is the whole frame, where copying only the area under the panels would save more on large windows.

Light: 2D lights

A 2D game looks flat until something is lit: a lamp’s warm circle in a dark room, a sign that glows. Painting a halo over the picture cannot do it, because light also has to darken what it does not reach. So lights work the way Unity’s 2D lights do: they are added together into a picture of light, which starts at an ambient colour, and then the frame is multiplied by it.

white := draw2d.Color{1, 1, 1, 1}
drawing.Image(room, draw2d.Rect{Width: 400, Height: 240}, 0, white)        // what is lit
drawing.PointLight(200, 120, 20, 80, draw2d.Hex("#ffeeaa"))                // full within 20 px, gone at 80
drawing.SpriteLight(signGlow, draw2d.Rect{X: 40, Y: 30, Width: 64, Height: 32}, white)
drawing.Lighting(draw2d.Color{0.6, 0.6, 0.8, 0.7})                         // the ambient: a dim blue
drawing.Text("Score 10", 8, 20, 16, draw2d.Medium, white)                  // after it: unlit

Lighting lights everything drawn before it, with the lights added since the last Lighting. What comes after it, such as the interface, keeps its own colours. Inside a clip (PushClip) it lights only the clip: a game’s view lit inside an editor leaves the panels around it as they are. A colour’s alpha is its intensity: an ambient of {1, 1, 1, 1} leaves everything as it is, and an alpha of 0.5 halves it.

Light can brighten too. Light is kept at half: 0.5 in the picture of light leaves a colour as it is, and 1 makes it twice as bright. The multiply counts the light twice (it blends the frame by the light and the light by the frame). So a lamp in daylight still shows, up to twice the brightness, with no extra cost.

How it is drawn. As with backdrops, the painter only marks where each Lighting falls (Painter.Lightings), and lays the lights out apart from the other quads (Painter.Lights). At each lighting the backend:

  1. clears a picture of light, at half the screen’s resolution, to the ambient;
  2. adds the lights into it, one square each. A point light’s shader fades it smoothly from its inner radius to its outer one, and a sprite light shines through its picture;
  3. draws the picture of light over the lighting’s area (its clip, or the whole frame) with the multiplying blend.

What it costs:

  • Nothing, without lights. A list with no Lighting draws as before.
  • Half the resolution for the picture of light, a quarter of the pixels: light is soft, and nobody sees the difference.
  • Three steps per lighting, however many lights it has: a clear, one draw call for all its lights, and a full-screen multiply.
  • The texture is kept, and made again only when the window changes size.

Measured as the backdrops were (llvmpipe, 1280 × 760), a frame of twenty panels takes 1.1 ms, and 3.0 ms with eight lights. A graphics card does the same work in a fraction of that.

OpenGL ES (Android and the web) draws them the same way, with what OpenGL ES 2 has: a texture to draw into and two blends.

The 3DS draws them too, in its own way (its guide).

Metal draws a flat interface’s lights the same way, and lights 3D scenes with point lights as OpenGL does. That code was written on Linux, where Metal cannot be built: it waits for a check on a Mac (go test ./metal compiles its shaders there). Lights in an overlay over a 3D scene are left unlit on Metal for now, like its backdrops there.
